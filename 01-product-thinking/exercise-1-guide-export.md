# Strategic Discovery Map (Module 1)

## Responses
- **Write one feature from the map that, if an engineer asked “why is this a priority for our 12-week roadmap?”, you honestly could not give a strategic answer for.:** Social leaderboards. Nothing in the map said who the user was, what the company was trying to achieve, or that anyone wanted to compete on water. It was there because "engagement apps have leaderboards", a feature-factory reflex rather than a customer need or business goal.
- **Write one feature that looks “correct” for the app but has zero strategic weight, you have no idea if it helps the company make money or whether a single user actually asked for it.:** A drink-type library with hydration percentages. It looks thorough and user-centred, but nobody asked for it, it doesn't touch the problem the map itself named (people forget to log), and it has no link to revenue or retention. It's organised, not prioritised.
- **Compare V3 to your Step 1 baseline. What changed, and is the output objectively better, did the AI get smarter, or did your added PM knowledge (JTBD, effort/impact, retention) force it to be useful?:** Same model, very different map. V1 was a feature list; V3 had a target user, a reason for each idea, a way to rank them, and named trade-offs (more reminders lift logging but drive uninstalls; a formed habit can mean churn). The AI didn't get smarter. The prompt carried the strategy (who it's for, JTBD, effort vs impact, retention), and the AI organised around it.
- **Key takeaway: how does this prove the PM’s role is to think while the AI’s role is to execute?:** V1 gave me features and V3 gave me decisions, and the only thing that changed was the product thinking I put into the prompt: the PM decides what matters, and the AI fills it in.
